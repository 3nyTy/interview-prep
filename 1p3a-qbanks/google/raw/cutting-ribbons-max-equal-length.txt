Cutting Ribbons (LC 1891)
LC 1891 Cutting Ribbons: given wood lengths and K, maximize the equal piece length that yields at least K pieces. Follow-ups cover the search bound, floating-point lengths, and why complexity depends on the value range rather than the number of elements.

Requirements

Given several pieces of wood with different lengths, cut at least K equal-length pieces and maximize that common length. Pieces cannot be joined across two source boards.

For a candidate length L, count sum(length // L for length in woods) and test whether the count is at least K.

Search the integer range [1, max(woods)], moving toward larger feasible lengths.

Follow-up: justify why the upper bound is max(woods) rather than sum(woods).

Follow-up: support floating-point lengths by searching a real-valued range with a fixed iteration count or precision threshold.

Follow-up: state the complexity as O(n log M), where M is the length value range, not O(n log n).

Notes

The interviewer allowed the candidate to abandon an initial greedy/sorting direction, then unlocked the problem with one question: what changes if a candidate length is supplied? Expect the follow-ups, not the base implementation, to consume most of the round.

For the integer version, initialize the best answer to 0, search only positive candidate lengths, and return 0 when even length 1 cannot produce K pieces. The feasibility predicate is monotone: once a length is infeasible, every larger length is also infeasible.

Preparation

Implement the integer version from memory and prove the monotonic feasibility predicate.

Rework it with floating-point lengths and explain termination under both fixed-iteration and epsilon-based policies.

Practice stating the bound and complexity in terms of the numeric value domain without conflating it with input size.
