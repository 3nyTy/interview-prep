Onsite System Design: Nearby Driver Proximity Service
Design a proximity service that ingests frequently changing driver locations and returns eligible drivers within a rider's search radius. The round compares uniform grids, quadtrees, and Geohash/S2; then probes boundary cells, four-second update cadence, city partitioning, driver-state filtering, and map-result debouncing.

Requirements

Ingest (driver_id, latitude, longitude, timestamp, status) updates from a large moving-driver population; each active driver may update about once every four seconds.

Given a rider position and radius, return nearby eligible drivers with low latency.

Filter out drivers who are already assigned or otherwise unavailable.

Partition the workload so ordinary proximity queries do not cross cities.

Keep current location state in the low-latency path while sending historical trajectories to a separate asynchronous store.

Stabilize results for the client map so small location changes do not make displayed drivers jump excessively.

Choose and defend a spatial-index strategy: uniform grid, density-adaptive quadtree, or Geohash/S2.

Notes

A uniform grid is simple but creates overloaded cells in dense city centers.

A quadtree adapts to density, but continuously moving drivers increase tree-maintenance cost.

Geohash/S2 represents locations with hierarchical cell identifiers. A radius query must enumerate every cell that can intersect the query area at the chosen resolution, then exact-filter candidate coordinates. The current cell plus its immediate neighbors is sufficient only when the cell geometry and requested radius prove that coverage; a fixed 3-by-3 neighborhood is not a general rule.

For a hierarchical hexagonal index, choose ring depth from the requested radius and cell resolution rather than hard-coding one ring.

Separate latest-location serving from trajectory retention: current state can stay in memory, while historical updates flow asynchronously to a time-series or analytical store.

Key follow-ups are spatial-boundary correctness, hot-city write pressure, city-level partitioning, availability filtering, and client-side/result debouncing.

Preparation

Draw the ingest, latest-state, radius-query, and history paths, then calculate write QPS from the four-second update interval under several assumed active-driver counts.

Practice comparing grid, quadtree, and Geohash/S2 on density skew, update cost, boundary handling, and distributed partitioning.

Put the rider near a cell boundary at two resolutions, derive the cells or ring depth needed for the requested radius, and verify that exact post-filtering removes out-of-radius candidates without dropping in-radius drivers.
