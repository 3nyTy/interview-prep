Culture Round — AI-Safety Alignment Behavioral
Anthropic's culture round is the round that decides the most loops. AI-safety alignment is screened through behavioral framing — interviewers want concrete personal experience that maps to specific Anthropic values, plus the ability to thoughtfully critique Anthropic itself.

Requirements

High-frequency prompts

Why Anthropic? Which specific value resonates and why?

What do you think of Anthropic's recent public positions / papers / policy stances? What would you push back on?

Tell me about a time you advocated for safety / risk over performance or speed.

A time you disagreed with a strong-willed collaborator on a non-technical issue and how you resolved it.

A moral dilemma you faced. How did you decide what to do?

A time you upheld a value under pressure, even when uncomfortable.

What's the most personally transformative thing that has happened to you? (Work or non-work.)

What would make you want to leave Anthropic?

What could Anthropic do better?

Something you pursue without any utilitarian payoff — in learning, life, or work — just because you care about it.

A conflict with a colleague where it later turned out you were the one who was wrong.

A position you strongly defended that later proved wrong — what changed your mind, and how did you respond?

The task you least enjoy doing — then convince the interviewer (who claims to dislike it too) why they should do it anyway.

Something you've done that you consider morally wrong, and how you reconcile it now.

A time you challenged authority or pushed back on a decision you disagreed with — concrete examples expected; personal-life examples are acceptable, not just work stories.

What success looks like

Specific Anthropic values cited (humility, care, candor, calibrated uncertainty, do-the-right-thing) — not generic "good culture."

Stories with measurable stakes (people, dollars, risk, time pressure) that demonstrate the candidate has personally lived the value.

Substantive critique of Anthropic — disagreement is welcomed when grounded in real tradeoffs.

6–10 polished stories rehearsed cold, with the candidate able to swap them between prompts.

Describe a strained relationship that you repaired.

Give a conflict rooted in values rather than only a technical disagreement.

Notes

The interviewer often runs out of time. Pace yourself — most successful answers are 2–3 minutes each, with explicit story → action → reflection structure.

Failure mode: reusing Amazon/Meta BQ stories without retuning. "Customer obsession" doesn't substitute for "do the right thing, even when costly."

Failure mode: agreeing with everything Anthropic does. Interviewers actively probe whether you can identify real tensions in Anthropic's strategy.

Several candidates report that the culture round was the deciding negative signal even when every technical round was strong-hire.

Style varies by interviewer: some run an explicit AI-safety/values interrogation, others keep it a relaxed open conversation that never mentions AI safety directly. The relaxed version is still scoring the same alignment signals through the stories you pick — treat "just chatting" as a graded round.

In senior / staff loops the culture round is sometimes run by a non-engineer, who leans harder on values-under-pressure and "talk me into / out of this" framings than on anything technical.

An interviewer explicitly rejected a set of purely technical conflict examples as insufficient and asked for a values-based conflict. Being persuaded by someone else and repairing a relationship are separate prompts; prepare both.

Preparation

Read Anthropic's published values (Responsible Scaling Policy, core values page, recent blog posts) and pick the three you actually believe in. Write a 2-paragraph personal story per value.

Prepare your "what could Anthropic do better?" answer specifically — substantive, not product-feedback. Examples that have landed: Anthropic's deployment pace vs. safety research throughput; transparency around RSP commitments; researcher/engineer split in hiring.

Practice the "what would make you leave?" answer. The honest, well-articulated version is the goal — not a non-answer.
